A thousand frames down to forty, in one sitting.

Pickture is for the part of photography that is deciding, not editing. You point it at a folder, move through it with the arrow keys, and the ones you keep are copied to a folder you choose. Your originals are never touched.

Why

The bottleneck is not editing. It is choosing.

Come home from a shoot and you have somewhere between three hundred and twelve hundred frames. Perhaps one in twenty is worth keeping. Before you can develop anything, you have to look at all of them and decide — and you have to do it fast enough that you can still hold a judgement in your head by the end.

The tools that do this well are expensive. The tools that are affordable are slow, or make you import a whole catalogue before showing you a single picture. So the culling either takes an evening it should not take, or it does not happen and the folder sits there.

Pickture does that one job. It opens a folder directly, shows you frames as fast as your machine can decode them, and gets out of the way.

The loop

Four keys, and your hand never leaves them.

Culling is a linear scan, so Pickture is built around one: navigate, judge, next. The whole loop runs from the keyboard, and the keys stay visible in the status bar so nothing lives only in a manual.

The filmstrip remembers what you decided

Every frame carries its judgement in shape as well as colour — a rail down the side of the cell, and a notch cut into the corner of kept frames. You can see where you are in a folder from the corner of your eye, without reading anything, and it works just as well if you do not distinguish those colours.

Frames you kept last time come back marked

Reopen a folder you have already worked on and the keepers are already flagged in selection/. Pickture reads the destination folder rather than trusting its own notes, so it is right even if you moved files around yourself.

Corrections

Four operations, so twenty keepers don't force a trip to Lightroom.

Pickture is not an editor and will not become one. But most frames that survive a cull need nothing more than a small correction, and doing that here saves opening anything else.

  • White balance on the value channel. Lifts a flat exposure while leaving hue and saturation exactly where they were.
  • White balance per channel. The one that removes a colour cast — each of red, green and blue stretched independently.
  • Levels. Black point, white point and gamma, dragged directly on the histogram. There is no Apply button: the picture changes as you move the handle.
  • Rotation. Quarter turns, which are lossless, plus a fine angle to straighten a horizon — cropped back to the original shape, so what you see is what gets written.

Everything is applied to a copy. The file you shot is opened, read, and never written to.

Speed

Why it feels immediate.

A photo tool lives or dies on the delay between pressing an arrow key and seeing the next picture. Most of Pickture's engineering is spent on that one number.

  • Thumbnails come from the JPEG already inside your file. Cameras write a small preview into the EXIF block; reading it is a seek rather than a decode.
  • They are cached on disk. Reopening a folder you have already culled is effectively instant, however large it is.
  • The next ten frames are decoded before you get to them. Culling moves in one direction, so the picture you are about to ask for is usually already waiting.
  • Pictures decode at the size of your screen, not the size of your sensor. A 24 megapixel frame has about twenty times more detail than a display can show.

The result on a 24 megapixel photograph: about 46 ms for a thumbnail and 75 ms for a full preview, on one core, with the rest of the work happening ahead of you in the background. Written in Rust, which is what makes that affordable.

Honesty

What Pickture does not do.

Better to know before you download it. These are refusals, not gaps waiting to be filled.

Not in Pickture Why
Raw files (ARW, CR3, NEF, DNG) Not yet, and I would rather say so than list formats it cannot open. JPEG, PNG, TIFF, BMP, GIF and WebP work today.
A catalogue or a library Pickture opens folders. Nothing is imported, nothing is indexed, and uninstalling it leaves your pictures exactly as they were.
Curves, masks, local adjustments, presets That is a photo editor, and good ones already exist. Four operations cover the frames that only need a nudge.
Cloud sync, accounts, sharing There is no server. Pickture never opens a network connection, so there is nothing to sign in to and nothing to leak.
macOS and Linux builds The code is portable and has no Windows-specific dependencies, but I only test on Windows. Building from source on the others should work.

Your files

Copies only. Never moves, never overwrites.

Keeping a frame copies it into a destination folder — selection/ beside your photos by default, or any folder you point it at, or a dated subfolder for a second pass. The destination is shown permanently next to the keep action, because it is the one setting that changes what lands on your disk.

If a file of the same name is already there, Pickture writes alongside it and says so rather than replacing anything. Your original files are opened read-only and are never modified, moved or deleted.

Who

Pickture is not a product. I take pictures — wildlife, and my family — and I wanted a tool for the boring half of that hobby that I did not have to fight, pay a subscription for, or wait on.
