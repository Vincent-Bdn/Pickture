Why this exists

I take photographs. Mostly wildlife, which means coming back with four hundred frames of the same bird and perhaps six that are sharp, and my family, which means the same problem with worse light and children who do not hold still.

Both produce the same evening: a card full of near-identical pictures, and a decision to make about every one of them before anything else can happen. That part is not hard. It is just long, and it is where the enjoyment of the hobby quietly goes.

So I went looking for something to help, and found the free tooling divided neatly into three kinds. The capable ones were expensive, priced for people who shoot weddings for a living rather than someone photographing herons at the weekend. The free ones were slow — wanting to import a catalogue before showing me a picture, then taking a second to load each frame, which is exactly the second that makes you stop. And the rest were hard to get a grip on: powerful editors where the job I wanted, look at a thousand pictures and choose forty, was buried somewhere inside a program built for something else entirely.

None of them were bad. They were built for other people. What I wanted was narrow: move through a long series quickly, mark what to keep, and have a small set of genuinely useful corrections so that the keepers which only needed straightening or a lift in the shadows never had to go anywhere else. That is a weekend's worth of ideas and rather more than a weekend's worth of engineering, and it is what Pickture is.

My background

I studied at the École polytechnique, then took a master's in mathematics and computer science at the École normale supérieure. I have written software ever since, and today I run Lysta, my company, which builds software for fraud detection and anti-money-laundering compliance.

That is worth mentioning here for one practical reason. Installing a free program from the internet is a small act of trust, and the honest answer to "who is this person" is better than any promise I could write. My working life is a compliance business, conducted under a company name, with clients who audit me. There is a name, a track record and a face behind this program rather than a pseudonym.

Pickture has nothing to do with Lysta and is not owned by it. It is a personal project with no customers, no invoices and no roadmap, written because I wanted the tool myself.

The question nobody asks out loud

What do I get out of it?

Nothing financially, and that is not about to change. There is no paid tier planned, no feature held back for a subscription, no data to sell — Pickture never opens a network connection, so there is nothing to collect even if I wanted to.

What I do get is real enough to be worth saying:

  • My own evenings back. I use this every time I come home with a full card. That was the point, and it remains the main one.
  • Something I am proud of that can be looked at. A public, carefully made program is worth more than a CV, for me and for my company.
  • The pleasure of building something properly, with no commercial trade-offs to make. That is a rare luxury and a good reason to keep going.

What I commit to, and what I don't promise

A free personal project does not behave like a software company, and pretending otherwise would misrepresent it.

I commit to I don't promise
Never modifying, moving or deleting your original files — and fixing anything that threatens that before anything else. Support by email or a guaranteed response time.
Saying clearly what the program does not do, rather than letting you find out. To build every feature that is asked for.
Keeping the source public, so anyone can read it, build it, and continue it for noncommercial use. A regular release schedule.
Keeping it free, with nothing held back behind a payment. To keep going indefinitely — but the code stays either way.

Getting in touch

For a bug or an idea, the issues page is best: it is public, so it is useful to whoever hits the same thing next. A free GitHub account is enough.

For anything else — including if you photograph something I have never thought about and the tool gets in your way — LinkedIn works well.
